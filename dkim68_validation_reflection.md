# Validation Reflection — dkim68

Going through my partner's ten projects showed me that most disagreements were not about the raw commit history itself. The harder part was deciding how to turn the same evidence into one label. ActivityPattern was the clearest example. A project can look both declining and irregular, but the validation works better when we choose the one pattern that best describes the whole timeline. After comparing the plots again, I usually focused more on the long-term direction instead of individual spikes.

Recovery was also harder to judge than I expected. A few commits after a long gap do not always mean the project fully recovered, but they do show that the project was not completely dead. The strongest recovery evidence was when commits after the gap had a clear purpose, such as compatibility fixes, Docker or CI work, dependency updates, bug fixes, or new features. This also showed that a long gap does not automatically mean project death. Some projects came back for maintenance even if they never returned to their old activity level.

Currentstatus was easier once we followed the coding rule exactly. Instead of judging whether a project "felt" inactive, we used the fixed cutoff date. That resolved cases where the project had very little activity but still had a recent enough commit. RecentThemes had a different problem: one commit can fit more than one theme, so disagreements often came from choosing the second most important category. Looking at several recent commits together was more useful than focusing on one message.

The biggest thing I learned from the validation is that qualitative coding needs consistent rules. Two people can look at the same project and both have reasonable explanations, but the final dataset is better when we use the same definitions and document why we chose a label. The discussion step helped turn vague judgments into decisions that were tied to the timeline, dates, and commit messages.
